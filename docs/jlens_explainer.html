The Jacobian lens, as you actually use it

Your lens asks one question of every decoder layer: given the vector sitting at the answer position right now, what yes/no margin is the rest of this network disposed to produce? It answers by differentiating through the remaining layers instead of guessing. This page teaches everything that sentence contains — the lens family it belongs to, the theorem that says its weights are a regression fit in disguise, the one statistic it actually reports, and the six places the implementation is weaker than the write-up — with live labs you can run in this tab.

Companion to the sparse-autoencoder explainer Numbers read from results_gemma/jlens_gemma/ and results_transfer/jlens/ Nothing on this page recomputes a published result

01 — The question

Where does a paraphrase flip get decided?

Two phrasings of one question, over one chest radiograph. Is there evidence of pleural effusion? and is there any pleural effusion? mean the same thing and carry the same answer. The model says yes to one and no to the other. Six decoder layers separate the tokens from that decision. Which of them changed its mind?

You cannot read the answer off the layers directly, because a residual stream is not written in the language of answers. It is a 384-dimensional vector that every layer writes into and every later layer reads out of. Anthropic's framing of this, which the rest of the page leans on:

“We generally think of the residual stream as a communication channel, since it doesn't do any processing itself and all layers communicate through it.”

“The residual stream has a deeply linear structure.”

Elhage et al., A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (2021), § Virtual Weights and the Residual Stream as a Communication Channel

A lens is any rule that turns one of those intermediate vectors into a prediction about what the network will eventually output. Every lens is a bet about the layers you are skipping over. The three that matter here make three different bets, and the differences are the whole subject of section 03.

The claim this method supports

From the repository README: “Across paraphrases, flipping clusters diverge about 8.9× more than stable ones, from layer 0, with a divergence-vs-flip correlation of 0.81.” Both numbers come from results_gemma/jlens_gemma/jlens.json. By the end of this page you should be able to say exactly what statistic each is, which model it was measured on, what it would take to break it, and — the part the README does not say — what the same measurement gives on the scaled model (2.2× and 0.26).

One thing to fix in place before anything else, because it decides what a lens can and cannot tell you here. The docstring of jlens.py already gets the ordering right, and it is worth keeping:

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyline 11
# The lens is corroboration only; the causal patch (experiment_e) is primary.

A lens is a reading. It tells you what a state looks like. It does not tell you that the state caused anything, because nothing was intervened on. The causal claim in this project comes from experiment C, which edits the activation and watches the answer move. What the lens adds is resolution: it is cheap enough to run on all 11,319 evaluation rows, where the patch runs on 20.

02 — One dot product

The decision is already linear, one layer too late

Start at the end, where there is no approximation at all. Baby-MedGemma does not have a classification head. It reads yes and no out of the tied input-embedding matrix, the way MedGemma does:

src/babygemma/gemma_model.pylines 92–95
h = out.last_hidden_state
pooled = h[torch.arange(B, device=h.device), ans_idx]
W = self.gemma.get_input_embeddings().weight          # tied LM head [vocab, dim]
logits = pooled @ W[[self.no_id, self.yes_id]].T      # [B, 2] = [no, yes]

So the yes/no margin — the single scalar this whole project is about — is a dot product of one 384-vector with one fixed direction:

margin(x) = logityes − logitno = hfinal · (Wyes − Wno)

That is worth sitting with. The model's answer is the sign of a projection onto a single direction in a 384-dimensional space. Everything the network does is in service of pushing one vector to one side or the other of the hyperplane orthogonal to Wyes − Wno.

The catch, which is not cosmetic

last_hidden_state is not the output of the last decoder layer. In HuggingFace's Gemma3TextModel the layer loop finishes and then a final RMSNorm runs. The margin is exactly linear in the post-norm vector, and the lens never sees that vector — jlens.py hooks model.gemma.layers[L], which fires before the norm. Verified against transformers 4.57.1 on the real module:

RMSNorm divides by the vector's own scale, so the margin as a function of the pre-norm layer-5 output is degree-zero homogeneous: multiply that vector by any positive number and the margin does not move at all. Which has a sharp consequence — the gradient of the margin is exactly orthogonal to the activation itself:

verificationscale invariance and the orthogonal gradient
margin(  0.25 * h5_pre) = +0.492862
margin(  1.00 * h5_pre) = +0.492862
margin(  4.00 * h5_pre) = +0.492862

cos(grad, h)            = -1.9e-09      # gradient is orthogonal to h, identically

Neel Nanda states the same fact for LayerNorm in the attribution-patching context, and it is the single most load-bearing caveat on this page:

“the derivative of LayerNorm normalisation is the complete opposite — the component in the direction of v is zero (because it scales v and its norm equally and cancels out) and the component in any direction orthogonal to v is unchanged.”

Neel Nanda, Attribution Patching: Activation Patching At Industrial Scale

Read the good news first. Because the margin ignores the length of the residual stream and cares only about its direction, the decision — the sign, the yes or no, every flip this project measures — is untouched by how big the vector is. The bad news is that a linear lens has no way to be blind in the same way. It reports J · h, which grows when h grows, and the statistic this experiment publishes is a spread of lens margins. Some of that spread is length, and length provably does not matter to the model. Caveat 3 puts a number on it.

03 — The lineage

Three lenses, and the bet each one makes

The idea of reading an intermediate state through the output head is nostalgebraist's logit lens, from 2020. Belrose and colleagues later wrote its assumption down in one line, which is the most useful sentence available for placing your method:

“The logit lens consists of setting the residuals to zero: LogitLens(hℓ) = LayerNorm[hℓ]WU.”

Belrose et al., Eliciting Latent Predictions from Transformers with the Tuned Lens (2023)

Setting the residuals to zero means: pretend the remaining layers do nothing. Apply the final norm and the unembedding early and see what falls out. It costs nothing to compute and it estimates nothing. It also breaks, and the breakage is documented rather than hypothetical:

“While this simple form of the logit lens works reasonably well for GPT-2, nostalgebraist (2021) found that it fails to extract useful information from the GPT-Neo family of models.”

“we find that for BLOOM and OPT 125M, the top 1 prediction of the logit lens is often the input token, rather than any plausible continuation token, in more than half the layers”

Belrose et al. (2023), § The Logit Lens

The diagnosis is representational drift: layer ℓ's activations simply are not written in the coordinate system the final unembedding expects, so reading them with that unembedding produces nonsense. The tuned lens fixes it by learning the change of basis — TunedLensℓ(hℓ) = LogitLens(Aℓhℓ + bℓ), with the pair (Aℓ, bℓ) trained by gradient descent to minimise KL against the model's own final distribution, not against the ground truth token.

Your lens takes the third road. It never assumes the bases match, and it never fits anything to a target. It differentiates through the remaining layers, which performs the change of basis by chain rule:

Logit lens (2020)

Apply the final norm and unembedding to hℓ.

Bet: layers ℓ+1…L are the identity.

Free. Nothing estimated. Fails when the bases drift.

Tuned lens (2023)

Learn an affine translator, then unembed.

Bet: the effect of the remaining layers is affine, and SGD can find it.

Trained against the model's final distribution. Scored by perplexity and bias.

Jacobian lens (here)

Average ∂margin/∂hℓ over examples; read the projection.

Bet: the effect of the remaining layers is affine, and the average derivative finds it.

One scalar, not a distribution. Nothing fitted, nothing validated.

On the axis the tuned-lens paper cares about — does the probe account for the remaining layers? — your lens is on the tuned lens's side. On the axis the name suggests — are the weights fitted or derived? — it is on the logit lens's side. The single cleanest way to say it: the Jacobian lens is the tuned lens's translator computed analytically instead of learned, collapsed onto a single output direction.

It also has a sibling on a different branch of the family, and that sibling is where section 08 ends up. Difference two phrasings' lens readings and the intercept cancels:

lensℓ(A) − lensℓ(B) = Jℓ · (hℓA − hℓB)

which is, character for character, the attribution patching estimator — (clean_act − corrupted_act) · grad — the standard first-order stand-in for a real activation patch. Not an analogy. The same algebraic object, with the per-example gradient swapped for a global average. That swap is not free, and caveat 4 is where the bill arrives.

The tuned lens earns its “more predictive, reliable and unbiased” claim with measured perplexity, measured bias, and causal experiments. The Jacobian lens as implemented reports no analogue of any of those. Inheriting the lineage does not inherit the evidence — and the cheapest way to earn some is caveat 2, which is four lines of code.

04 — Calculus = regression

The average gradient is a fitted probe in disguise

It is tempting to say the Jacobian lens gets its weights “from calculus rather than from fitting”. That is a nice line, and there is a theorem that makes it much more interesting than a nice line — by mostly dissolving the distinction.

Write gℓ(h) for the scalar map from a layer-ℓ activation to the final margin. The best linear predictor of the margin from the activation — ordinary least squares — has slope β = Σ−1Cov(h, g) and prediction m̄ + β·(h − h̄). That is structurally the same formula the lens uses. And by the multivariate Stein lemma, if h ~ N(μ, Σ) then Cov(h, g) = Σ·E[∇g], so

β = Σ−1 Σ E[∇g] = E[∇g] = Jℓ

Under Gaussian activations the Jacobian lens is the least-squares linear probe — same slope, same intercept, no approximation. Calculus and regression are the same estimator. They come apart only to the extent the activations are not Gaussian, and residual streams are famously not: the tuned-lens paper's own diagnosis of logit-lens failure is that “transformer hidden states often contain a small number of very high variance dimensions”.

So the honest version of the slogan is better than the slogan. The lens is a linear probe whose coefficients are estimated from the model's derivatives instead of optimised against a target, and the gap between that and a fitted probe is exactly the non-Gaussianity of the activations. The lab below lets you open that gap and watch it.

Lab · Stein's identity, and where it stops holding average gradient against fitted OLS, on the same samples

A synthetic g(h) = φ(w·h) in d dimensions with a smooth nonlinearity, sampled n times. We compute the average gradient J = E[∇g] and the fitted OLS coefficient β from the very same draws, then compare them. Heavy tails pushes the activation distribution away from Gaussian; curvature makes g less affine.

cos(J, β)—1.00 = identical direction
R² of the lens—average-gradient weights
R² of OLS—fitted weights, same data
‖J‖ / ‖β‖—scale agreement
At zero heavy tails the two agree to sampling noise — that is Stein's lemma. Push the tails up and the directions separate while OLS keeps its fit, which is the precise sense in which “calculus instead of fitting” is a real but bounded distinction. Note also what curvature does to both: no linear readout survives a strongly non-affine g, however its weights were obtained.
Why this matters for the defense

A committee member who asks “why should an average gradient be a good readout?” is asking a question with a real answer: because it is the population OLS coefficient whenever the activations are jointly Gaussian, and an estimator of it otherwise. That is a stronger position than “it's the derivative, so it must be informative”, and it names its own assumption, which is the part that makes it defensible.

05 — Your recipe

Fifty lines, and what each of them commits you to

The whole method is two functions. The first fits the lens; the second reads it. Nothing else in jlens.py touches the model.

Fitting: three expectations, one backward pass each

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyfit_jacobian, lines 39–84 (abridged)
def fit_jacobian(model, dataset, device, n_fit=256):
    # hook every decoder layer and keep its output tensor, with grad retained
    for L in range(depth):
        def hook(mod, inp, out):
            hs = out[0] if isinstance(out, tuple) else out
            hs.retain_grad()                      # keep d(margin)/d(hs)
            captured[L] = hs

    loader = DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=1, collate_fn=collate)
    for b in loader:
        if n >= n_fit: break                     # <- 256 rows, in dataset order
        logits, _ = model(vis, tokens, ans_pos)
        margin = logits[0, 1] - logits[0, 0]      # yes minus no
        margin.backward()
        ai = int(b["ans_pos"][0]) + model.n_img
        for L in range(depth):
            Jsum[L] += captured[L].grad[0, ai].detach()   # the Jacobian
            Hsum[L] += captured[L][0, ai].detach()        # the mean activation
        msum += float(margin.detach()); n += 1
    return [Jsum[L]/n ...], [Hsum[L]/n ...], msum/n, depth

Three quantities per layer: Jℓ the average gradient, H̄ℓ the average activation, and m̄ the average margin. Note that margin.backward() is a scalar backward through the whole network — this is what makes the lens account for the remaining layers rather than assuming them away.

Reading: one projection per layer per example

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyread_lens, line 114
lm = mbar + ((h - Hbar[L]) * J[L]).sum(-1)
m̂ℓ(x) = m̄ + Jℓ · (hℓ(x) − H̄ℓ)
Two of the three fitted quantities never affect a published number

Every statistic this script reports is a within-cluster standard deviation of m̂, or a correlation built from one. Both m̄ and H̄ℓ are constants across examples, so they cancel exactly: stdc(m̂) = stdc(Jℓ·hℓ). The ratio and the point-biserial are additionally invariant to the scale of Jℓ, so the entire published result depends on nothing but the direction of the average gradient.

This is good news and worth saying out loud at the defense. It means the results carry no dependence on the expansion point, no dependence on whether H̄ is even a realisable activation, and no dependence on the Jacobian's magnitude. It also means the “first-order Taylor expansion” framing is doing less work than it appears to — see caveat 5.

The fit set is smaller than it looks

n_fit=256 reads as a comfortable sample. It is not 256 questions. The evaluation set is built cluster by cluster — one original phrasing plus 48 paraphrases from src/babygemma/templates.py, so 49 consecutive rows per question — and the fit loader is created without shuffle:

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyline 61
loader = DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=1, collate_fn=collate)   # no shuffle=True

So the first 256 rows are clusters 0 through 5, and the “average over 256 examples” is an average over six distinct images and questions, each contributing 49 near-duplicate phrasings. Out of 231 clusters on the probe, and 1,500 on the scaled model.

06 — A flip, close up

What the lens sees when one question disagrees with itself

A cluster is one image and one finding, asked 49 ways. The 48 paraphrases come from src/babygemma/templates.py, twelve in each of four registers, and every one of them is meaning-preserving — the file is explicit that answer-inverting rewrites like “is {f} absent?” live in a separate bank and never enter a cluster.

The flip rule is in src/babygemma/metrics.py and it is as blunt as it sounds — a cluster flips if its 49 predictions are not unanimous:

src/babygemma/metrics.pyflip_labels, lines 51–55
return {c: len(set(ps)) > 1 for c, ps in by.items()}

Now put the two together. Each phrasing produces a margin. The cluster's answer is unanimous exactly when all 49 margins land on the same side of zero. So a flip is a race between two quantities: how far this question's margin sits from the boundary, and how much the phrasing moves it. The lens measures the second one directly — that is what lens_divergence is, the within-cluster standard deviation of m̂.

Push the mean margin toward zero and the cluster flips even at the stable spread; push it far out and even the flipping spread stays unanimous. This is why flip rate is a property of the pair — a model can be made to look robust by being confidently wrong, which is exactly the failure the AUC analysis elsewhere in this project catches. Then switch the run to the scaled model and toggle the cluster type: on the probe the two types are unmistakable (1.94 against 0.22), while on the scaled model they are 0.031 against 0.014 and both types flip at the same distance from the boundary. That is what 8.9× versus 2.2× feels like.
What is real here and what is drawn

The per-layer spreads, the flip/stable contrast, and the ratios are read from the committed result JSONs. The individual 49 traces are sampled from those published statistics — jlens.py computes per-example lens margins in read_lens but only ever writes the aggregate to disk, so per-phrasing traces do not exist in the repository. Nothing on this page pretends otherwise, and caveat 11 is the two-line change that would make them real.

Three readings, in descending order of how comfortable they are.

Read one: the separation is real, and it is not marginal

On the probe, flipping clusters' lens margins spread 8.87× wider than stable ones at layer 0, and the per-cluster spread correlates with flip status at 0.809 over 231 clusters. Even the weakest of the six layers survives a Bonferroni correction for all six by forty-five orders of magnitude. Nothing about this is a threshold effect or a marginal p-value. The scaled model reproduces the sign and the direction at 2.23× and 0.265 — much weaker, still far from chance.

Read two: the curve is flat, so the lens localises nothing

lens_commit_layer is defined as the shallowest layer where flipping clusters diverge more than twice as much as stable ones:

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyline 171
commit = next((L for L in range(depth) if div_flip[L] > 2 * div_noflip[L] + 1e-6), None)

It returns 0 for both runs. But the criterion is already satisfied at the first layer measured and stays satisfied everywhere, so “layer 0” is a floor, not a finding — the lens cannot report anything shallower because it has nothing shallower hooked. The honest statement is the separation is fully present at the output of layer 0 and is merely carried forward, which is a real and interesting result, but it is not a localisation.

And on the scaled model the threshold is fragile

The probe's ratios run 8.57–9.77, so the > 2× test passes with enormous margin at every layer. The scaled model's run 2.23, 2.02, 2.02, 1.99, 1.97, 1.95 — layer 0 clears the threshold and layers 1–5 sit just under it. Raise the constant from 2 to 2.1 and lens_commit_layer becomes None. The reported value is robust on the probe and one decimal place from flipping on the scaled model; say so before someone asks.

Read three: the correlation gets weaker with depth, which is the wrong direction for an artifact

The obvious objection to any lens result is circularity: the final-layer lens margin approximates the real margin, and a cluster flips exactly when the real margin changes sign across phrasings, so of course late-layer spread predicts flipping. If that were the whole story the point-biserial would climb toward the readout. It falls, monotonically, on both runs — 0.809 → 0.769 on the probe and 0.265 → 0.231 on the scaled model. The most predictive layer is the one furthest from the head. That is the opposite of what circularity predicts, and it is the strongest single point in the method's favour.

08 — Lens vs patch

The cheap estimate and the real intervention

The lens says the phrasings' dispositions separate at layer 0. Experiment C says something stronger and different in kind: take the answer-position activation from a phrasing the model got the majority answer on, write it into the flipped phrasing's forward pass, and the answer comes back. That is causation, not correlation.

The two are not independent methods, and it matters to say so. Difference the lens across the donor and target and you get precisely the attribution-patching estimate of what the patch will do:

predicted margin change = Jℓ · (hℓdonor − hℓtarget)

And the patch experiment C runs really is the full intervention that estimates. Its “rank-1 difference-direction” patch, with the basis taken along d − t, collapses algebraically:

t − (t·b)b + (d·b)b = t + ((d−t)·b)b = t + (d − t) = d   where b = (d−t)/‖d−t‖

Verified numerically to 3.6e−07. So the answer-position activation is completely replaced at that layer; the rank-1 framing earns its keep only by making the random-direction control a fair comparison. This tightens the correspondence to attribution patching — and it also means the patch sits squarely in the regime the attribution-patching literature warns about:

“It works best when patching things near the end of the model, and when making 'small' patches, where patch represents a small fraction of the residual stream, and badly for big patches (eg an entire residual stream).”

Neel Nanda, Attribution Patching

Both curves are at their ceiling, and one of them should not be

On the probe, recovery is 1.00 at layers 0 through 4 with a random-direction control at 0.00; on the scaled model it is 0.83–1.00 against a control never above 0.10. A saturated curve cannot localise a layer any more than a flat one can, so “the lens and the patch agree on layer 0” is two methods both reporting the shallowest point of a curve that never varies. The defensible joint claim is narrower and still worth making: the flip is already decided at the output of the first decoder layer, and it is carried in a single direction at the answer position — which the random-direction control at 0.00 does establish, and which is what the dissertation's language-side argument actually needs.

The layer-5 dip is almost certainly an artifact, and it does not touch the finding

At the last layer the patch should be deterministic. Replace the answer-position vector with the donor's, and the only thing between it and the logits is the final norm — recovery must be exactly 1.000. It is 0.60 on the probe and 0.72 on the scaled model.

The reason is the hidden_states convention from section 02. experiment_e.py harvests its donor from acts[L], and acts[5] is post-final-norm, while the patch hook writes into the pre-norm stream. The final norm then runs a second time. RMSNorm is scale-invariant so the size mismatch is harmless, but the learned (1+w) gain is applied twice, which rotates the vector. Simulated on isotropic activations, that costs a few percent of decisions in general — and far more for donors near the boundary, which flip-cluster donors are by selection:

How Anthropic frames the same tension

Attribution graphs are built from a linearised replacement model, and the Circuit Tracing paper is direct about what that costs and what it requires: “Our approach to studying circuits is indirect — our replacement model may use different mechanisms from the underlying model. Thus, it is important that we validate the mechanisms we find in attribution graphs. We do so using perturbation experiments.” They also report the decay honestly, finding “reasonably good predictive power for effects a few layers downstream of a perturbation, and much worse predictive power many layers downstream.” The posture to copy is not that linear approximations are trustworthy — it is that they are cheap, and that you say out loud which intervention checked them.

09 — Caveats

Eleven things about this implementation, and the fix for each

None of these overturn the finding. Most are one- or two-line changes, and several would make the result stronger to state. They are ordered by how much they affect a number or a figure that has been published.

Ordered by blast radius click to expand
1The concept figure is hand-drawn, and its shape contradicts the measurement printed beside itfigure

figures/jlens_concept.png is not plotted from data. The curves are literals in scripts/legacy/figures.py:

scripts/legacy/figures.pylines 108–110
xs  = list(range(7))
yes = [0.0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.4, 0.75, 1.0, 1.1]
no  = [0.0, -0.04, -0.12, -0.35, -0.7, -0.95, -1.05]

It draws two phrasings starting together and separating around layer 2.6 — the README's caption says they “track together early, then commit to opposite answers”. Two paragraphs below it, the same README reports the measurement: 8.9× apart at layer 0, and the measured curve is flat. The figure also has seven x-positions for a six-layer model. A committee member who reads the picture before the sentence will form exactly the wrong mental model, and the sentence will look like a correction.

Fix. One word in the caption — call it a schematic — or replace it with the real per-layer curve, which tells a better story anyway: the phrasings never track together at all. Regenerating a published figure two weeks out is the riskier of the two; the caption is free.

2Experiment C's layer-5 donor is post-final-norm; the patch writes into the pre-norm streamnumber

experiment_e.py harvests donors from acts[L], which is out.hidden_states[1:]. HuggingFace appends the hidden state before each layer runs and then appends the final-normed state after the loop, so the last entry is normed and the rest are not. Verified on transformers 4.57.1: acts[L] == hook(layers[L]) for L = 0–4, and acts[5] == norm(hook(layers[5])).

At layer 5 the patch should be deterministic and recovery should be exactly 1.000. All 18 baby-MedGemma runs report 0.273–0.817. All 16 retired-nanoVLM runs under results/E/ — a decoder with no post-loop normalisation on the captured state — report exactly 1.000. The double-applied (1+w) gain explains the split, and it bites hardest for near-boundary donors, which flip-cluster donors are by construction. Layers 0–4 are unaffected, and that is where the finding lives.

Fix. Capture donors with a forward hook on layers[L], exactly as jlens.py already does, instead of reading acts[L]. Changes recovery_rank1_by_layer[5] only. Post-defense — but have the diagnosis ready, because the dip is visible in every E figure.

3The published divergence includes activation length, which the decision provably ignoresnumber

The margin is degree-zero homogeneous in the pre-norm activation — scaling it by 100 leaves the margin bit-identical, and the gradient is orthogonal to the activation to 1.9e−09. The lens reads J·h, which is not scale-invariant. So part of every reported lens_divergence is variation in ‖h‖ across phrasings, and that variation cannot change any answer.

Paraphrases differ in token count and content, which is exactly the perturbation that moves the norm, so the contamination is not hypothetical. It inflates flip and stable clusters alike, so the ratio is partially protected — which is another reason the README is right to quote 8.9× rather than the absolute divergence.

Fix. Read the lens on the RMS-normalised activation, or project J onto the subspace orthogonal to h̄. One line, and it would move 8.87 and 0.809 — so this is post-defense. State the direction of the bias if asked: it affects both cluster types, so the ratio moves less than the absolutes.

4Nothing in the pipeline checks that the lens reading resembles the real marginmissing

The tuned lens earns its claims with perplexity and bias measured against the model's true final distribution. This lens reports no analogue. Without one, “lens” is doing unearned work — we are asserting that m̂ℓ tells you something about m without ever having compared them.

read_lens already computes the true logits — it needs them for preds — and throws the margin away.

scripts/experiments/jlens.pyline 108, the two lines to add
preds = logits.argmax(-1).cpu().tolist()
true  = (logits[:, 1] - logits[:, 0]).cpu().numpy()   # add
# then per layer: np.corrcoef(lens_margin[:, L], true)[0, 1]

Fix. Two lines, no retraining, adds a new number rather than moving an old one. This is the single highest-value addition on the list: it converts “the lens is corroboration” from a posture into a measurement, and gives you a direct answer to the obvious question. Safe to run before the defense on a fresh output directory.

5“256 examples” is six questions, taken in order, from the set the lens is then read onmethod

DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=1, collate_fn=collate) at line 61 has no shuffle, and make_eval_clusters emits 49 consecutive rows per question. The first 256 rows are clusters 0–5. The Jacobian is therefore an average over six images and six findings, out of 231 or 1,500 — and those same rows are inside the set the lens is later read on, so the fit is in-sample.

Mitigating: the quantity being averaged is a derivative of the model, not a fit to labels, so in-sample-ness cannot leak flip information the way a supervised probe would. The narrow sample is the real issue.

Fix. shuffle=True on the fit loader — one keyword, turns six questions into 256. Fitting on the validation split instead is a three-line change. Both alter J and so would move published numbers: post-defense.

6It is not a first-order Taylor expansion, and two of its three fitted quantities cancelwording

A Taylor expansion about H̄ would be g(H̄) + ∇g(H̄)·(h − H̄) — the function and the gradient evaluated at H̄. The code uses E[g(h)] and E[∇g(h)], both dataset averages, which differ by curvature. The correct name is an average-derivative estimator, and by Stein's lemma it is the population OLS coefficient under Gaussian activations — a stronger and more interesting statement than the Taylor one, as section 04 works through.

Relatedly, m̄ and H̄ are constants across examples and cancel exactly in every reported statistic. The results depend on the direction of J and nothing else. That is a robustness property worth claiming, not a defect.

Fix. Wording only — no number moves. Say “average-derivative estimator” or “gradient-fitted linear probe”, not “Taylor expansion”, and note the cancellation as a strength.

7The gradients are averaged with their signs, which both attribution-patching papers deliberately avoidmethod

Syed et al. take the absolute value per pair and then the mean. AtP* is explicit about why: taking the absolute value inside the expectation “decreases the chance that estimates across prompt pairs with positive and negative effects might erroneously lead to a significantly smaller estimate.” fit_jacobian sums raw signed vectors, so if ∇m points in opposing directions across examples, ‖J̄‖ collapses while every individual gradient is large.

Note this is the right thing to do for a lens — a lens needs one global linear map, and averaging signed gradients is how you get the OLS estimand. It is the wrong thing for an importance score. The two uses have been conflated in the write-up.

Fix. A diagnostic, not a change: report per layer the mean cos(∇mi, J̄) and ‖J̄‖ / E‖∇mi‖. High values earn the single-global-map claim outright. Adds numbers, moves none.

8lens_divergence_by_layer is not comparable across layers, or across the two modelsreporting

The absolute divergence is std(Jℓ·h), which scales with ‖Jℓ‖. That norm differs by layer, so a rise across depth can be the Jacobian growing rather than the phrasings separating. It also differs by model: the probe's divergences sit near 2.0 and the scaled model's near 0.045, a factor of 45 that says nothing about robustness.

Fix. None needed for the published claims — the README already quotes the ratio, which is scale-free, and the point-biserial, which is scale-free. Just never put the two models' absolute divergences on one axis, and prefer the ratio for any cross-layer plot.

9The commit layer is left-censored, and on the scaled model it is one decimal from vanishingreporting

commit is the shallowest layer clearing div_flip > 2 * div_noflip. The condition holds at every layer of both runs, so the answer is the first layer hooked. Nothing shallower is measured — the token embedding and layer 0's own sublayers are inside the black box — so “layer 0” is a floor.

On the probe the ratios are 8.57–9.77 and the verdict is unambiguous. On the scaled model they are 2.23, 2.02, 2.02, 1.99, 1.97, 1.95: layer 0 clears the threshold and the rest sit just below. Change the constant from 2 to 2.1 and the reported commit layer becomes None.

Fix. Hook hidden_states[0] (the embeddings) and the attention/MLP sublayer outputs, so layer 0 becomes a measurement rather than the edge of the instrument. Report the ratio curve alongside the commit layer so the threshold's role is visible. Neither moves an existing number.

10The lens and the patch it corroborates were run on different checkpointsprovenance

Probe: jlens at 1,500 steps (early-stopped 750, acc 0.887, flip 0.0996) against experiment C at 2,000 steps (early-stopped 225, acc 0.897, flip 0.0866). Scaled: 8,000 steps against 12,000. Same recipe, same regime, same seed, different budget.

For the qualitative claim — both methods point at the early layers — this is adequate, and both models sit in the same behavioural regime. For any regression of predicted against measured margin change it is not: that comparison needs one checkpoint and one scalar.

Fix. Run both from a single --model-steps in one driver. The two scripts already share train_model, so this is a scheduling change, not a code change.

11One seed, no error bars, and the per-example readings are computed then discardedcoverage

Experiments A and B run 8 and 24 seeds. jlens.py ran once, at seed 0, for each model. The 8.9× and the 0.81 have no interval attached to them.

Separately, read_lens builds a per-example, per-layer table of lens margins — rows, with cluster id, phenomenon, prediction and the six readings — and analyze reduces it to eight aggregate arrays. The table is never written out, which is why the traces in section 06 had to be sampled from the published statistics rather than plotted from a log.

Fix. Seeds 0–7 with mean and standard deviation of the ratio, matching the rest of the project — this is compute, not code, and it adds an interval rather than changing the point estimate. And one np.save of rows, which unlocks per-register breakdowns, the faithfulness check in caveat 4, and real traces for the figure.

Four of these (4, 7, 8, 9) add numbers without moving any. Two (1, 6) are wording. The rest change a published value and belong after the defense.

The one experiment that would settle it

Everything above is housekeeping next to a single missing measurement. Syed et al. validated attribution patching by scattering its prediction against the true effect of the patch it approximates, and reported the fit honestly:

“Surprisingly, we find a fairly weak correlation between activation and attribution patching scores (R² = 0.27). Further, the line of best fit has gradient 0.531, suggesting that attribution patching estimates the effect of activation patching as twice as important as it really is.”

Syed, Rager & Conmy, Attribution Patching Outperforms Automated Circuit Discovery (2023), §5.1

You are three small changes from the same plot on your own model. _patch_pred in experiment_e.py already runs the patched forward pass and discards the margin, keeping only int(logits.argmax(-1)). Return the margin instead. Compute Jℓ·(hℓdonor − hℓtarget) for the same donor–target–layer triples on one checkpoint. Scatter the two and report R² and slope.

If that R² beats 0.27, the lens stops needing a defense and becomes a validated instrument with a published benchmark behind it. If it does not, you will have found that out yourself, quantified, with the framing already in place — which is a far better position than having a committee find it. Either way the causal result is untouched, because the patch is measured, not estimated.

Cost

The lens itself is cheap. fit_jacobian is 256 forward-and-backward passes at batch size 1, and read_lens is one forward pass over the evaluation set at batch 128 — 11,319 rows on the probe, 73,500 on the scaled model. Training the model beforehand dominates the wall clock by a wide margin, and the frozen MedSigLIP features are read from cache, never recomputed.

A last note on what to say if the lens is challenged directly. It is not an independent corroboration of the causal patch — it is the first-order approximation of that same patch, and a first-order approximation agreeing with the thing it approximates is a consistency check. The independent legs of the argument are the intervention itself, whose random-direction control sits at 0.00, and the unsupervised decomposition in the sparse-autoencoder explainer, which recovers the same direction without ever being shown a flip label. The lens's job is resolution: it is the only one of the three you can afford to run on every row.
