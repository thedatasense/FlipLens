An interactive primer on the uncertainty and gating results

Knowing when not to answer

A model that flips its answer when you rephrase the question is dangerous only if it answers anyway. So the practical question isn't whether flips happen. It's whether the model can tell you, in one forward pass, that this particular answer shouldn't be trusted. Two signals say yes. A third thing the model needs to know, whether it read the image at all, has no such signal, and that turns out to be the finding that matters.

01 — Two failures

A wrong answer and a blind answer look nothing alike

There are two ways this model fails you, and they need different alarms.

The two failure modes, and what catches each
FailureWhat it looks likeSingle-pass signal?
Paraphrase flipSame image, same question reworded, opposite answerYes. Entropy and margin both rank it well.
Blind answerConfident answer that ignored the radiographNo. It's at chance, 0.47 to 0.52.

The first one has a tidy story. A flip is a sign change of the yes-minus-no margin across phrasings, so it's likeliest when that margin already sits near zero. Any signal that tracks how close the answer is to the boundary will rank flip risk. Both signals here do exactly that, and they do it well.

The second one doesn't. A confident answer that never read the image looks identical, from the outside, to a confident answer that did. Same margin, same entropy, same everything. You only catch it by running a second pass with a different patient's image and seeing whether the answer moves. That's an offline check. It can't run in a clinic.

Where this ends up

Chapter 7 builds a rule that tries to admit only predictions that are both stable under rephrasing and dependent on the image. On the targeted adapter it admits 33.0% of questions at 96.8% accuracy, against 91.5% if you answer everything. That reads like a win. It isn't. About 93% of what it admits is exactly what the question text alone already answers, so the rule is mostly certifying the model's text prior. Section 07 walks through it.

02 — Entropy

One number, from one forward pass

The model gives a probability to yes and a probability to no. Predictive entropy measures how close that pair is to a coin flip. Low entropy means the model committed. High entropy means it nearly didn't.

The claim in Chapter 7 is that this single number ranks both errors and paraphrase flips. It costs nothing, because you already ran the forward pass to get the answer.

Three signals, one ranking

From the source data's own note: softmax entropy, temperature-scaled entropy and absolute margin are rank-equivalent on this task. So one signal is what all three report. That's worth saying out loud, because it means you can't improve the ranking by switching between them.

03 — The margin gate

The same idea on the controlled probe

Section 02 is the deployed model. This is the small probe from Chapter 5: a Gemma-3 decoder over a frozen MedSigLIP-448 encoder, reading a single yes or no from its own language-model head. Its only inference-time signal is the yes-minus-no margin.

The detector gets better on hospitals it never saw. That looks strange until you see why. Under a distribution shift the whole margin distribution moves, and more answers land near the boundary, which is where flips live. The signal isn't learning the hospital. It's reading distance to the boundary, and that quantity means the same thing everywhere.

This detector also beats re-asking the question under several paraphrases, and beats a trained hidden-state probe, at no extra inference cost. That's the practical claim, and it holds.

04 — The blind spot

What the margin can't catch

Everything so far ranks one failure: the answer that changes when you rephrase. There's a second failure, and the same signal is useless against it.

The number that matters

The absolute margin separates flipped from stable answers at 0.923 to 0.974. Against an answer that ignored the image, the same margin sits at 0.47 to 0.52. That's chance. A confident answer that never read the radiograph looks exactly like a confident grounded one.

You can only catch it with a second pass. Swap in another patient's image and see whether the answer moves. If it doesn't, the first answer wasn't reading the picture. That check needs a second image known to carry the opposite label, so it's an offline evaluation tool. No clinic can run it on a live case.

A finer question, and a clean null

Suppose the answer stays the same under rephrasing, but the model quietly changes how much it leans on the image. Does that happen? At the population level, yes. Per case, you can't identify it. The measurement is a null with a bound attached:

QuantityValue
Grounded-to-unreliant transitions observed0
Patient-seed evaluations345
Patient-level upper boundnear 3%

Zero events in 345 evaluations doesn't mean the rate is zero. It means the rate is small, and the bound is what you can defend. Say it that way. A null result with a stated bound is a finding. A null result reported as "we saw none" invites the obvious follow-up about power.

So per-case image reliance is a population property here, not a per-prediction label. That single sentence carries most of the deployment argument, and section 07 is where it bites.

05 — Where it commits

That control is what makes the result mean something. Without it, "transplanting things changes the answer" would be unsurprising.

How this differs from the small probe

The 6-layer FlipLens probe puts everything at layer 0, because a rank-1 patch works at every layer it can reach and the lens separation is already there at the first layer measured. Six layers is too few to localise anything. MedGemma-4B has 34, and the transition from 0.2% to 100% inside seven layers is a real localisation. When you present both, lead with this one. The lens page explains why the probe's version is a floor rather than a finding.

06 — The audit

Four conditions, and what each one buys

Entropy can't tell you whether the image was used. So Chapter 7 combines several signals and asks the rule to admit only predictions that are both stable under rephrasing and dependent on the image. Toggle the conditions and watch what each one does.

This is an audit, not a deployable gate

The region condition needs a radiologist's bounding box around the finding, which by definition doesn't exist for a patient nobody has read yet. It's available for 637 of these 861 questions and for none of MIMIC. The swap condition needs a second image known to show the opposite label. Both are offline checks. Neither could run in a clinical workflow.

07 — Why it fails

The gate certifies the text prior

Look at the last column of that table. On the base model and the targeted adapter, about 93% of everything the rule admits is a question the model answers correctly from the text alone, with no image at all.

So the high accuracy on admitted cases isn't evidence of grounding. It comes from high finding base rates making the text prior usually right. The rule is admitting the easy questions and calling them safe.

You can see this directly in the lab. Turn on the fourth condition, the one that isn't in the published rule, and require that the question not be answerable from text alone:

Targeted LoRA, recomputed from the 861 rows. Adding the text condition is what collapses it.
RuleAdmittedAccuracy on admitted
No gate100%91.5%
Paraphrases agree only89.7%98.6%

Twenty questions out of 861, at 55% accuracy. That's a coin flip on the only slice where the image had to do the work.

The finding of Chapter 7

On the slice where the model's text-only answer disagrees with its full answer, the targeted adapter scores 2.9% accuracy (n = 241), and Qwen2-VL gets 0 of 279 right under every rule tried. The dissertation's answer to "can a gate admit image-grounded cases?" is no.

That's the strongest result in this chapter, and it's a negative one. Negative results defend well when the control is clean, and this one is: the rule was given every signal, including two that need a radiologist and a second image, and it still couldn't find grounded cases. Lead with that framing rather than apologising for it.

One thing to have ready. The 2.9% and n = 241 figures come from a different artifact than the 861-question file behind the lab above, so the two don't line up on their own. Section 08 has the detail.

08 — Caveats

Seven things to have an answer for

None of these change the conclusion. Most are a caption or a second number. They're ordered by how likely a committee member is to hit them.

Ordered by how exposed they are click to expand
1The 2.9% grounded-slice figure doesn't reproduce from the file behind the demonumber

The prose says the targeted adapter scores 2.9% accuracy on n = 241 where the text-only answer disagrees with the full answer. Apply that same definition to gate.json, the 861-question PadChest flip bank the interactive runs on, and you get a different slice:

ModelSlice sizeAccuracy
Base MedGemma-4B1998.0%
Targeted LoRA8716.1%
Full LoRA20458.8%

No combination of the fields in that file gives 241. So the two numbers describe different populations, and the stated one comes from a fuller evaluation than the demo bank. That's fine. What isn't fine is putting them on the same screen without saying so, because the first question will be "which 241?"

Fix. One clause in the caption naming the population behind 2.9% and n = 241, the way the other panels already name theirs. Both directions of the result agree, so nothing about the argument changes.

2The swap condition is partly a finding-level property, not a per-case onewording

The checkbox reads "Swapping in an opposite-label image changes the answer", which sounds like a fact about this prediction. The rule string in the data is broader: it admits when the swap changed the answer, or when this finding responds to image swaps across the bank, or when the region and background disagree.

That middle clause is a property of the finding, not of the case in front of you. It makes the condition easier to satisfy, and it means a given admitted prediction may never have been swap-tested itself. Given that the chapter's whole point is that image reliance isn't a per-case label, this is the one place the labelling works against the argument.

Fix. Label the checkbox with the full disjunction, or report the admission rate with and without the finding-level fallback. The numbers don't move; the claim gets more precise.

3Questions without a radiologist's box can only be admitted by the swap conditionmethod

The region condition is coded 0 for all 224 questions that have no box. Zero means "the region and the background agree", which is the same code as a real negative test. So a missing measurement and a failed measurement are indistinguishable inside the rule.

In practice, 44 of the 284 questions the published rule admits on the targeted adapter have no box, so they got in on the swap condition alone. That's about 15% of admissions resting on one signal instead of two.

Fix. Report the rule on the 637 boxed questions as a sensitivity check. If admission and accuracy hold there, the missing-box coding cost you nothing and you can say so.

4Entropy's error ranking falls to near chance on one of the three modelsreporting

The headline pair, 0.823 for flips and 0.862 for errors, is the targeted adapter. Across all three models the error column moves a lot more than the flip column:

ModelFlipsErrors
Targeted LoRA0.8230.862
Base MedGemma-4B0.6920.857
Full LoRA0.7910.569

0.569 is nearly a coin flip. Full LoRA still ranks flips at 0.791, so the two abilities aren't the same ability, and full fine-tuning breaks one without breaking the other. That's a result, not an embarrassment, but only if you're the one who points it out.

Fix. Quote the model with the number, every time. Add one sentence saying flip ranking and error ranking come apart under full fine-tuning.

5The margin is a much better flip detector than error detector, and only the flip number is quotedreporting

Against paraphrase flips the margin scores 0.923 in distribution and 0.974 on both held-out hospitals. Against answer correctness, the same signal on the same rows scores 0.715, 0.647 and 0.645.

That's a coherent story rather than a problem. A flip is literally a sign change of the margin, so a margin-based detector should be excellent at flips and merely decent at errors. Saying it first makes the 0.974 look understood instead of lucky.

Fix. Add the error column to the margin table with the one-line explanation. No number moves.

6The image-reliance chance range has no sample size attachedreporting

"0.47 to 0.52" is the whole claim that the margin can't detect blind answers, and it's doing a lot of work. It's quoted without an n, without a confidence interval, and without saying which sets the two endpoints come from.

An area under the curve of 0.47 on a small set is consistent with a real effect. On a large one it's a clean null. Nobody can tell which from the number alone.

Fix. Give the range an n and an interval per set. If the interval straddles 0.5 comfortably, the null gets stronger, and this becomes a sentence you want to be asked about.

7The near-3% bound depends on a patient count that isn't statedreporting

Zero grounded-to-unreliant transitions over 345 patient-seed evaluations, bounded near 3% at the patient level. The standard bound for zero events is roughly 3 divided by the number of independent units, so 3 over 345 evaluations gives 0.87%. Reporting near 3% instead means the bound was computed over roughly a hundred independent patients rather than over 345 correlated evaluations.

That's the conservative and correct choice, and it's invisible from the sentence. Right now it reads like a looser bound than the data supports, which is the safe direction to err, but only if you can say why on request.

Fix. State the patient count alongside the 345, and say the bound is computed at the patient level to account for repeated seeds. That converts a vague number into a defensible one.

Caveat 1 is the only one that touches a printed number, and it's a caption. Everything else adds a column or a sample size.

What to say when asked whether any of this is deployable

The honest summary is short, and it's stronger than a hedge.

QuestionAnswer
Can one forward pass rank flip risk?Yes. 0.823 on the deployed model, 0.974 on held-out hospitals for the probe.
Can it rank errors?On two of three models, yes. Full fine-tuning breaks it.
Can it tell whether the image was read?No. Chance, per case, with no known single-pass substitute.
Can a rule admit image-grounded cases?No. Given every signal, including two that need a radiologist, it admits the text-answerable ones.

The last row is the contribution. A gate that works by certifying the text prior is worse than no gate, because it produces a number that looks like safety. Showing that cleanly, with the controls to back it, is worth more than a gate that scored well.
